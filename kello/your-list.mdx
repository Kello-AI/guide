Your Kello list arrives as a link to your list page with up to 20 people who fit your role. Each person comes with a highlight, the reasons they fit your role, and one thing worth checking, and you mark each one Yes, Maybe or No.

If not enough people meet the bar, Kello sends fewer, tells you how many it evaluated and which requirement is narrowing the search, and you never get an empty list.

Every highlight and reason comes from the candidate's own profile or Kello's data on companies and people. Kello doesn't invent them.

## How does the list arrive?

As a link to your Kello list page, usually about two hours after you send the role. You can share the link with anyone involved in the hire.

## What does each candidate show?

Each person on the list has:

- **Name, current role and company, location and experience.**
- **A highlight.** What stands out about this person.
- **Why this candidate.** The reasons this person fits *your* role, written in plain words.
- **Worth checking.** One thing to verify before or during a first conversation.

"Why this candidate" is written for your role, not a generic summary. The same person would read differently on another company's list.

## How do I mark candidates?

Mark each person **Yes**, **Maybe** or **No**. Add a short note on why if you can: `too senior`, `love this one`, `wrong industry`.

Your marks do two things:

1. **They decide outreach.** Kello reaches out to everyone you mark Yes, then everyone you mark Maybe. Never anyone you mark No.
2. **They shape the next list.** Kello studies your Yes and No profiles in depth to learn your team's taste. Notes make the reasons explicit.

See [Feedback and next lists](/kello/feedback#how-do-i-mark-candidates) for more.

## What does "how Kello approached your search" show?

The list page also explains Kello's thinking:

- How it read your role.
- What it learned from your past hires.
- The kinds of people it looked for.
- What it relaxed, and why.

<Tip>
Read this first. If Kello misread something, reply with the correction (`it's a Director role`, `Pune, not Bangalore`) and Kello restarts on the corrected brief.
</Tip>

## Are these people looking for a job?

Most aren't actively looking. Nobody on your list applied, and nobody is contacted until you mark them Yes or Maybe.

## What if I already know someone on the list?

Tell Kello and they won't be contacted, or simply mark them No. If they're already in your hiring process, say so.

## Can I share the list?

Yes. Share the list link with your hiring manager or anyone else involved. Anyone with the link can review and mark candidates. See [how to share a list with your team](/kello/team#how-do-i-share-a-list-with-my-team).

## Can I download the list?

Yes. Download it from the list link.

If your list is short:

## Why are there fewer than 20 people?

Because not enough people met the bar for your role. Each list has **up to 20** people, not exactly 20.

When a list is short, Kello tells you which requirement is narrowing the search. It might be a minimum years of experience, a single city, a budget, or a specific industry. Only you know which of these is truly firm.

### Why doesn't Kello fill the list anyway?

Because a padded list wastes your time. Kello would rather send you 14 strong people than 20 with six who don't fit.

## How does Kello report a short list?

Kello tells you how many people cleared the bar out of how many it evaluated, and asks which requirement can bend. A typical note reads:

> This list has 7 candidates instead of the usual 20. **7 of the 312** candidates evaluated cleared the bar under the current constraints. Tell me which constraint can bend and I'll widen the next pass.

Read the two numbers together. "7 of 312" means Kello looked at plenty of people. The pool isn't thin; your requirements, taken together, are tight.

## What happens if nobody fits?

You never get an empty list. Kello works through it in this order:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Kello checks its own assumptions">
    If an assumption Kello made is the cause, such as reading a nice-to-have as a must-have, Kello adjusts and searches again.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Kello asks you one clear question">
    If one of your requirements is the cause, Kello asks you about it directly. For example: "Is the 8-year minimum firm?"
  </Step>
  <Step title="Someone from the Kello team steps in">
    If the search is still stuck, a person from the Kello team works on it with you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What should I do with a short list?

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read why it's short">
    The list page explains what Kello relaxed and what is narrowing the search.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide which one requirement can bend">
    Reply in plain words. One clear change beats several vague ones.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Mark the people you have">
    Yes, Maybe or No, with a note. Your marks shape the next list even when this one is short.
  </Step>
</Steps>

```text Example replies wrap
8 years is flexible. 6+ is fine if they've led a team.
Open to people willing to relocate to Mumbai, and the range can go to 35 LPA.
Budget can stretch 15% for the right person.
Fintech is a nice-to-have, not a must-have.
```

LPA: lakhs per annum, yearly pay in Indian rupees (1 lakh = 100,000).

Kello's next list usually arrives within a few hours of your feedback.

## Can I ask what narrowed the list?

Yes. Reply:

```text wrap
What constrained this?
```

Kello lists the requirements doing the narrowing and says which one would widen the search most. You then decide which can bend.

<Callout icon="quote-left" color="#9b5d98">
**Real example: 4 to 20 after widening location and budget.** A recruiter got a list of 4 and asked what constraints led to it. Kello named the three requirements narrowing the search and the biggest lever. Kello replied: *"Opening up location to candidates willing to relocate…"*

The recruiter opened the role to people willing to relocate and raised the budget. The next lists went from 4 to 5 to a full 20, then 11 strong candidates, and the recruiter marked 8 Yes.
</Callout>

## How do requirements stack up?

Each requirement can be reasonable on its own. Combined, they narrow the pool fast. These are real searches, anonymised:

| Requirements combined | People who cleared the bar |
| --- | --- |
| One metro area only + one sector only + a segment excluded + 9–15 years | 2 of 214, then 1 of 298 |
| Tier-1 college + graduated by a set year + Series A/B startup background | 7 of 312, 12 of 249, 7 of 405 |
| Enterprise healthcare sales to large hospital systems + a fixed pay band | 2 of 477 |
| Local candidates in one small district + a low pay band | 3 |

The fix is the same each time. Label every requirement <Badge color="blue">Must</Badge> or <Badge color="gray">Nice</Badge> in your brief, so Kello knows what it can relax. When a list comes back short, reply with the one requirement that can bend.

## Will Kello tell me if the brief doesn't match the market?

Yes. When a brief asks for something the market rarely has in one place, Kello says so plainly instead of padding the list.

<Callout icon="quote-left" color="#9b5d98">
**Real example.** For a corporate M&A search that wanted deal origination from a set of named firms, Kello replied: *"most M&A professionals at these specific firms are focused on transaction execution, while those with the 'origination' [skill set] are typically found in Investment Banking…"*
</Callout>

That's your cue to choose: widen the companies, or drop the requirement.

## Does a role in two cities get two lists?

No. A role open in two cities is one role. List both locations and the list stays up to 20 people across both. If you want a full list for each city, send each city as a separate role.

## How do I avoid a short list next time?

- Label each requirement Must or Nice in your brief.
- Say up front which requirements can bend.
- Share a few LinkedIn profiles of people you'd hire.

See [Write a great brief](/kello/write-a-great-brief#why-split-must-haves-from-nice-to-haves).
